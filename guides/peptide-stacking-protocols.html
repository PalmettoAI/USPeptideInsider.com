Peptide stacking protocols: pharmacologic logic, not message-board folklore

"Stack" is a marketing word for combination pharmacology. Most published peptide stacks are reasonable. Some are redundant. A small number are unsafe. This guide is the pharmacologic decision tree behind the choices, not a list of regimens.

USPI Editorial / Updated 04.20.26 / 16 min read

The decision tree, in three questions

Every defensible stack answers three questions explicitly. Most internet stacks fail at question 2.

  1. Are the compounds acting on different mechanisms? If two peptides drive the same receptor, you usually want one — at the right dose — not both at half doses.
  2. Is the combined dosing pulsatile-physiologic, or sustained-supraphysiologic? The somatotrophic axis tolerates pulsatile elevations; sustained elevations downregulate.
  3. Is there a cross-mechanism contraindication? Angiogenic + proliferative + active malignancy is the canonical bad-combination pattern.

Repair stacks (BPC-157 ± TB-500)

The most common repair stack is BPC-157 with TB-500 (thymosin beta-4 fragment). The two compounds act on different mechanisms — BPC-157 primarily through VEGFR2/NO, TB-500 primarily through actin sequestration and stem-cell migration — so they pass question 1.

Practical observations from the field literature:

  • Co-administration is usually parallel, not premixed — different stability profiles after reconstitution
  • Subcutaneous near-site is the most-cited route for tendon and connective tissue indications
  • Total daily dose: BPC-157 250–500 µg + TB-500 2–5 mg, with TB-500 typically loaded weekly rather than daily

For the underlying compound profile see our BPC-157 entry.

GH-axis stacks (CJC-1295 + Ipamorelin)

The pulsatile GH-axis stack is covered in detail in the CJC-1295 / Ipamorelin profile. The short version: GHRH-analog activity (CJC) and ghrelin-mimetic activity (ipamorelin) are pharmacologically synergistic at the somatotroph; the combination produces a meaningfully larger GH pulse than either alone with retention of physiologic pulsatility.

The mistake to avoid here is stacking the DAC and non-DAC forms of CJC-1295 simultaneously. The DAC form produces sustained, near-flat GH/IGF-1 elevation; layering pulsatile dosing on top of a sustained agonist defeats the pulsatility argument that justifies the stack in the first place.

Metabolic stacks (GLP-1 ± peptide adjuncts)

The legitimate FDA-approved metabolic peptides — semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide — do not require stacking to produce their indicated effects. The literature on adjunctive peptides (e.g., AOD-9604, MOTS-c) for body composition does not support meaningful additive efficacy in the registrational data we've reviewed.

One exception worth flagging: the combination of GLP-1 agonism with a GH-axis secretagogue is theoretically interesting because the lean-mass loss observed in GLP-1 monotherapy is partially rescued by IGF-1 elevation. The clinical data is currently animal-model and small open-label only.

The combinations to actively avoid

  • BPC-157 + active oncology: VEGFR2 upregulation in any context with proliferative tissue is contraindicated
  • Multiple GHRP-class compounds simultaneously: GHRP-2 + ipamorelin produces redundant GHS-R1a agonism with cortisol baggage you don't need
  • DAC and non-DAC CJC-1295 layered: defeats the pulsatile rationale
  • Vasoactive peptides + active cardiovascular instability: PT-141, AOD-9604, and others have hemodynamic effects worth respecting

Sourcing implications for stacks

The more compounds in a stack, the higher the cumulative sourcing risk. Each additional vendor relationship is another opportunity for substitution, contamination, or simple identity failure. The pragmatic answer is to consolidate to one or two audited US-based vendors.
