FOUNDER STORY · About 10 minutes

By Jack & Lila Carver

How We Got Here — A Letter About Why We Write These Letters

Dear Reader,

This is the letter that explains the rest of them.

If you have read Letter #1 or Letter #2 or Letter #3 and wondered who the hell is writing this and why they're writing it as if they have lived inside the exact body you are living inside right now — this is where we tell you who we are and how we got here.

It is a long answer. We promise it is the right one. We do not believe in founder stories that are mostly marketing. We believe in founder stories that explain why a person is the right person to be writing what they are writing — and we are going to do our best to do that here, the same way we would tell it to you over dinner if you asked us.

The Three Years We Don't Talk About Often

Lila stopped sleeping through the night somewhere around forty-three.

Not in a dramatic way. Not in a 3 a.m. wide-awake-pacing way. In the quieter, harder-to-describe way of falling asleep at 10:30, waking up at 4:42, lying in the dark for an hour, falling back into a thin sleep, waking up exhausted at 6:30, and pretending it was fine because it had been fine the previous night. Then the next night happened. Then the next.

For the first eight months, she didn't tell Jack. She told herself it was stress, that work was busy, that she had had a glass of wine at dinner two nights in a row and that was probably it.

Around the same time, Jack started getting sore from workouts in a way he didn't recognize. He had been lifting and running on and off since college, and recovery had been the easy part. You wreck yourself Saturday, you're back by Monday. That stopped working. He went out on a Saturday morning easy run and was sore in his hamstrings and lower back for five days. He did a Tuesday lifting session and his shoulders ached through the weekend. He told himself he just needed to warm up better, sleep more, take collagen, change his shoes, lose five pounds. None of it worked.

For the first eight months of his version of this, he didn't tell Lila. He told himself it was stress, that work was busy, that he was probably forty-five and just needed to accept it.

We were married. We lived in the same house. We had been together for fifteen years. And we both spent the better part of a year quietly, separately, deciding the body we were now living inside was just what aging felt like, and not telling each other.

That part of the story is the part most people don't write down. We are writing it down because almost every couple we have talked to about this — almost without exception — has done some version of the same thing. Aging happens privately first. It happens in mirrors at 6:42 a.m. It happens in gym bags you stopped unpacking. It happens in glasses of wine at 9 p.m. It happens in scheduled physicals that come back "normal."

It does not happen at the dinner table where you can hear it.

Until, eventually, it does.

The Conversation That Changed Things

It was a Sunday night, about two and a half years ago. We had eaten an early dinner. We were sitting on the back porch. Lila said, more to herself than to Jack, that she could not remember the last time she had slept through the night.

Jack said, slowly, that he could not remember the last time he had finished a workout and not been wrecked by it.

We sat there for a minute.

Then Lila said: "Are you doing this thing where you're not telling me how bad it is."

We talked for about three hours that night. We compared notes. We were astonished by how identically our bodies had been failing in different rooms for nearly a year. We were also, in retrospect, a little angry — at ourselves, at our doctors, at the entire wellness internet that had spent the last decade telling us to be grateful for our morning routines while none of the morning routines fixed any of the actual problems.

We made a quiet decision that night that turned out to matter. We decided we were going to take this seriously, and we were going to take it seriously together.

We didn't know what that meant yet. But we knew the secrecy had been the worst part of it.

The Years of Reading

We are not doctors. We are going to keep saying that throughout this site, because it matters. Lila has a graduate degree but it isn't in medicine. Jack worked in finance, then operations, then writing — none of which qualifies him to give you medical advice and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

What we did have, between us, was the ability to read carefully and the ability to be relentless about it.

The first thing we read was the menopause literature. Lila started there because that is what was happening to her, and what almost no general-practice physician we had spoken to seemed to know anything about. The book that changed her thinking earliest was Jen Gunter's The Menopause Manifesto, and the one that gave her the language for what was actually happening was Mary Claire Haver's The New Menopause. Neither was the final word — neither claims to be — but together they gave Lila the basic biological vocabulary for perimenopause that her own gynecologist had not given her in three appointments.

Jack started in a different place. He started with Peter Attia's work on longevity and the four horsemen of disease. He read Outlive twice. He read the deep-sleep and growth-hormone literature. He spent six months reading endocrinology papers he didn't fully understand and re-reading the parts he did. He learned the word somatopause, which is the men's version of menopause and which almost no men have ever heard of, despite living through it.

Within a few months, our two paths converged on the same place — the framework underneath both of our experiences was the same framework. Different sex hormones, same downstream collapse. Same growth hormone collapse. Same insulin sensitivity drift. Same loss of deep sleep. Same visceral fat redistribution. Same brain fog. Same decline in recovery. We were two halves of the same biology.

We started calling it The Repair Window, between us, before we ever wrote a word of it down for anyone else. The 25 years between 35 and 60 during which the body's overnight repair machinery — the peptide signaling, the deep sleep, the growth hormone cascade — quietly stops working the way it used to. Once you have the framework, every individual symptom stops looking random. They are not random. They are the same Window expressing itself a dozen different ways.

What We Tried, In Order

We are going to tell you what we did, in roughly the order we did it, because we think the order matters. We tried the cheap, free, repeatable things first. We saved the more interesting stuff for later. Most of the work was the cheap and free stuff. The interesting stuff just polished it.

First, we changed when we ate dinner. We pushed it from 8 or 9 p.m. to 6 or 6:30. Within two weeks, both of us were sleeping better. Lila was getting through the night again, most nights. Jack was waking up less stiff.

Second, we cut the evening wine. Not forever — we still drink — but we cut it Sunday through Thursday, and we cut it altogether for two weeks at the start to see what happened. What happened was a noticeable improvement in deep sleep, measured on the wrist, and a much sharper morning.

Third, we started the 90 seconds of cold at the end of the shower. It was awful for two weeks. Then it was tolerable. Then it was the part of the shower we looked forward to. The deep sleep numbers continued to climb.

Fourth, we started lifting. Twice a week, forty-five minutes, four basic lifts, adding a little weight every week. Jack went back to a gym. Lila set up a small barbell setup in our basement. Within four months, Jack's recovery from runs was back to forty-eight hours instead of five days. Lila's body composition started shifting visibly. We were not transformed. We were no longer collapsing.

Fifth, we ran a real lab panel. We covered the seven tests in Letter #3. The results both surprised us and didn't. Both of our IGF-1 levels were in the bottom third of the range. Lila's free testosterone was below the female reference range entirely. Jack's fasting insulin was in the early-warning band. Vitamin D was low for both of us. None of these would have been flagged by a standard physical. All of them were quietly contributing to how we felt.

Sixth — and this is the part where we want to be honest with you in a way that founder stories usually aren't — yes, eventually we explored the peptide therapies that gave this site its name. Through a licensed clinician who specializes in this area. Under supervision. Lila took a low-dose growth hormone secretagogue stack for a defined period. Jack ran a similar protocol on a different timeline. We will write about both of those experiences in detail in later letters, with specifics, with what worked, with what didn't, with side effects, and with the honest cost picture. We are not going to pretend we didn't try them. We are also not going to recommend them to you in this letter, because that is not what this letter is for, and because peptide therapy is a real medical intervention that requires a real clinician.

The order of the things we tried was deliberate. The boring stuff is what did the most work. The interesting stuff polished what the boring stuff had already done.

Why We Started Writing

We started writing because of a friend.

About a year and a half ago, a close friend of ours — a man in his early fifties — sat down with Jack at a bar one night and described, almost word-for-word, the body Jack had been living in two years earlier. The exhaustion. The recovery. The shape of him in photographs that no longer matched the person inside the photograph. He was telling Jack because he was scared, and he didn't have anyone to tell.

Jack told him what he and Lila had spent two years figuring out. He told him in plain English. He told him about the Window and the hormones and the labs to ask for and the order to try things in. The friend asked him to write it down. Jack went home that night, sat at the kitchen table, and started writing the letter that would eventually become Letter #1.

Six weeks later, Lila joined the writing. Some letters are mostly Jack's voice. Some are mostly Lila's. Most of them are both.

We started posting on Instagram a couple of months later, for the same reason — we figured if one friend at a bar needed it, so did some larger number of people we did not know. We chose Instagram because the audience we wanted to reach was already there, scrolling at 11 p.m. when they couldn't sleep. The handle is @HealthierLivingDaily. It's the daily, short version of what these letters are the long version of.

This site is the long version. It is going to keep growing for years. We will write a letter every week or two — sometimes about a specific symptom, sometimes about a specific compound, sometimes about a specific question we keep getting asked. We will not write filler. We will not write to a content calendar. We will write the next thing that needs to be written for the audience we are accumulating.

What We Won't Do

A few quick promises so you know what you're getting.

We won't run sponsorships. The letters will not contain paid placements. If we ever recommend a product or a clinician, we will say why, we will say whether we benefit financially (sometimes we will — affiliate links from companies we already recommend are how a lot of small writing operations stay viable), and we will tell you the alternatives.

We won't sell a course called The Repair Window Method. We won't sell a 30-day program. We won't put up a sales funnel or a lead magnet or a tripwire. The whole site is free to read. There are no email signups. There never will be. (If you want the daily version, that's what Instagram is for.)

We won't pretend we are doctors. We are not, and we are uninterested in playing that role. Where the letters discuss medical interventions — peptides, hormone therapy, prescription compounds — we will be specific that those interventions live in a relationship with a licensed clinician, not in a relationship with us.

We will, eventually, sell something. Probably a long-form guide. Probably a small set of clearly disclosed affiliate recommendations for things we already use and pay for ourselves. When we do, we will write a letter about it. You'll see it coming. You'll be able to decide.

That is most of who we are.

What This Site Is For

The site is for the version of us we were three years ago, awake at 4:42 a.m., not telling each other how bad it had gotten, and waiting for someone to write down in plain English what was actually happening to our bodies.

Nobody was writing that. So we are writing it.

If a letter on this site lands for you the way that conversation in the bar landed for Jack's friend — that's the whole point. Read the next one. Forward it to one person. Find us on Instagram. Try the boring shifts. Run the labs. Don't make any of this a religion. Don't burn your weekends on it. Don't spend money you don't have.

Just don't do what we did, which was spend a year and a half quietly suffering in different rooms in the same house because the system that was supposed to name what was happening had told both of us we were fine.

You are probably not fine. You are inside a Window.

The Window is open right now.

We are glad you are here.

Thank you for being here.
