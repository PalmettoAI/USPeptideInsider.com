Why Your Labs Are "Normal" But You're Not — A Letter

Dear Friend,

You sat across from your doctor a few weeks ago, told her — or him — that you had not felt like yourself in three years, and watched as she opened your chart on her laptop and slowly nodded.

"Everything looks fine."

"Your numbers are within range."

"You look healthy on paper."

You drove home from that appointment feeling something specific that we need to name, because most people feel it and never name it: a flat, hollow grief. You had walked in carrying real symptoms — exhaustion, weight you couldn't lose, the body that ached for no reason, the stranger in the photographs — and you had walked out being told that, according to the system designed to identify what was wrong with you, nothing was wrong.

You went home. You questioned yourself. You wondered if maybe you were imagining it. You wondered if maybe you were just getting old. You wondered if maybe other people felt this way too and were just better at handling it than you were.

None of those things are true.

What is true is this: the standard panel of blood tests your doctor runs at an annual physical was not designed to catch what is happening to you. It was designed to catch acute disease — cancer markers, severe deficiencies, organ failure — and that's what it does well. The slow hormonal collapse that defines The Repair Window is invisible to the standard panel, because the standard panel doesn't measure most of the right things, and the things it does measure get compared to a "reference range" that is wider than it should be for an adult who actually wants to feel well.

This letter is the one that tells you what to ask for next time.

The Reference-Range Trap

Before we tell you what tests to ask for, you need to understand the single most damaging concept in routine American lab medicine: the reference range.

When a lab result comes back and the doctor tells you it's "normal," what they actually mean is: it falls within the reference range. The reference range is the range of values that the middle 95% of people who got tested at that lab fell into. Your number is compared to the population that came through the same lab and got tested. If you're within the bell, you're "normal."

Pause and reread that sentence, because the implication is huge.

The reference range is not a range of healthy values. It is a range of what other people who got tested were measuring at. And the people who get blood tested at large reference labs are not, on average, healthy. They are the population of Americans who go to doctors — which means a population that includes a lot of people in early metabolic decline, walking around exhausted, gaining visceral belly fat, and quietly feeling exactly the way you feel.

Your "normal" is being compared to their "normal." Their "normal" is, on average, kind of a mess.

The result is that you can have a thyroid number, a testosterone number, a vitamin D number, an inflammation number — pick any of them — that falls comfortably within the reference range, and yet sit in a place that, for an adult who wants to feel well, is functionally suboptimal. You are not flagged. The number is not bolded in red. The doctor does not raise an eyebrow. And yet the chemistry of your body is sitting somewhere it doesn't have to be sitting.

The fix for this is not to chase exotic tests. The fix is to know which tests to look at, and to look at where in the range your number sits — not whether it is in the range.

The Seven Tests Worth Asking For

If you only had time to ask for seven specific blood tests at your next appointment — and you had to do it without sounding like you read a long internet article — these are the seven we would ask for, in the order we would ask for them, and roughly what you would want them to look like for someone aiming to feel well inside The Repair Window.

This is not medical advice. We are not your doctor. The numbers below are general orientation, not personalized targets. But these are the tests we wish someone had asked us about ten years ago.

1. IGF-1.

This is the single most important test almost no one runs. Insulin-like growth factor 1 is the downstream marker of growth hormone — the chemistry that drives your overnight repair. Growth hormone itself is hard to measure (it pulses), but IGF-1 is a stable marker that reflects the average. If your IGF-1 is in the lower third of the reference range for your age, growth hormone is almost certainly low — which explains the tired wake-up, the slow recovery, and the visceral fat that won't move. Most doctors will not run this unless you ask. Most labs will run it for $50–$80 if you order it directly.

2. Free testosterone (not total).

Total testosterone is what most doctors run. Total testosterone is mostly meaningless. The vast majority of testosterone in your blood is bound to a carrier protein (SHBG) and not biologically available. Free testosterone is what your tissues actually use. A man can have a "normal" total testosterone of 550 ng/dL and a free testosterone in the bottom 10% of the range — feeling exactly like a man with low testosterone, because functionally, he has low testosterone. Same for women, who run on much smaller amounts of free testosterone but absolutely run on it. Ask for free, not total. If your doctor says "we don't usually run that," that is informative. (For women: ask for free testosterone, SHBG, and DHEA-S — the trio gives a much better picture than estradiol alone.)

3. SHBG (sex hormone binding globulin).

The protein that binds your sex hormones. High SHBG means even if your total testosterone or estradiol looks normal, very little is biologically available. SHBG climbs with age and with chronic stress. Knowing your SHBG transforms the interpretation of every other hormone result on your panel. It costs almost nothing to add. Doctors rarely add it.

4. Fasting insulin.

Almost every doctor will run fasting glucose. Almost no doctor will run fasting insulin. This is the most consequential omission in routine American lab medicine, because fasting insulin rises a decade before fasting glucose rises. By the time your fasting glucose is "borderline," you have been insulin-resistant for ten years. Fasting insulin under 6 µIU/mL is great. Fasting insulin between 6 and 10 is the early-warning band. Above 10 means your body is already working hard to keep glucose normal — and that work is what's putting visceral fat on your belly. We covered the visceral-fat connection in detail in Letter #2.

5. hsCRP (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein).

An inflammation marker. Not perfect, but sensitive enough to give you a meaningful number for chronic low-grade inflammation. hsCRP under 1.0 mg/L is great. Between 1.0 and 3.0 is a warning. Above 3.0 means systemic inflammation is meaningfully elevated — and chronic inflammation is the upstream driver of almost every Repair-Window symptom you have, including the brain fog, the joint aches, the slow recovery, and the cardiovascular risk profile that climbs as you age.

6. The full thyroid panel — not just TSH.

Most doctors run thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and call it a thyroid panel. TSH alone is incomplete. A full panel includes TSH, free T4, free T3, reverse T3, and thyroid antibodies (TPO and TgAb). What you want is to actually measure the thyroid hormones — not just the pituitary signal that's supposed to drive their production. It's possible to have a "normal" TSH and a low free T3, which produces every symptom of hypothyroidism while looking fine on paper. The antibodies test for autoimmune thyroiditis (Hashimoto's), which the standard panel completely misses. Anyone with persistent fatigue, hair thinning, cold hands, or unexplained weight gain should be running the full panel, not just TSH.

7. Vitamin D (25-hydroxyvitamin D).

The reference range starts at 20 or 30 ng/mL depending on the lab — meaning anything above that is "not deficient." Functional ranges for adults inside The Repair Window are higher than that — many endocrinologists target 40–60 ng/mL. Below 30 ng/mL is associated with worse mood, worse sleep, worse immune function, and worse insulin sensitivity, even though the lab will not flag it as abnormal. Cheap to measure. Cheap to fix. Almost every adult we know who tested came in lower than they expected.

Run those seven, look at where in the range your number sits (not just whether it's in the range), and you will have a meaningfully more honest picture of what's happening inside your body than you got from the panel your doctor ran last year.

What To Say When You Ask

You don't need to walk in with a medical-jargon checklist. You need to say something specific.

Try this: "I've been feeling [exhausted / unrecovered / not myself] for a while now and the standard panel hasn't shown anything. Before we conclude there's nothing going on, would you be willing to run these seven tests with me? I want to understand the full picture of what's happening hormonally and metabolically."

Hand them the list.

You will get one of three responses. We have seen all three.

Response 1: They run the tests. Best case. Most internal-medicine doctors and family-medicine doctors will, especially if you're not making it adversarial. They may push back on one or two ("we don't usually do reverse T3," "fasting insulin isn't standard for your age"), but most will run most of them.

Response 2: They explain why they won't. Acceptable, sometimes correct. There are insurance reasons, there are clinical-judgment reasons, and there are some tests that genuinely don't add value for some patients. Listen to the reasoning. If it's "we don't run that because the standard panel is what we run," that's not really a reason — that's institutional inertia.

Response 3: They get defensive or dismissive. Informative. Doctors who get defensive when a patient asks specific, reasonable questions about their own labs are not the doctors you want for a Repair-Window-era body. The whole point of being inside this transition is that you need a clinician who is curious, who reads, and who treats you like a partner. If you got Response 3, you got a piece of information about your doctor — and the next step is to find a different one. Look for terms like "functional medicine," "integrative medicine," "longevity-focused primary care," or "menopause specialist." A NAMS-certified menopause specialist will run the full panel without flinching.

If Your Doctor Won't Run Them, You Can Run Them Yourself

This is something nobody told us about until we were two years into our own search for answers, and we want to make sure you know it: in the United States, you can order most of these blood tests for yourself, without a doctor's order, for less than $250 total.

Direct-to-consumer lab services — Quest's MyLabsDirect, Labcorp's OnDemand, plus several independent platforms — let you choose individual tests, pay out-of-pocket, walk into a draw site, and get results emailed to you within a few days. The tests are run at the same labs that hospital doctors use. The results are exactly the same.

You can run all seven tests above for somewhere between $150 and $300, depending on which service. We are not going to recommend a specific service in this letter — the right one for you depends on your state, and the landscape changes — but if you search "direct to consumer blood tests [your state]" you will find three or four legitimate options.

Once you have results, you can take them to any clinician — your existing doctor, a new doctor, a NAMS specialist, an endocrinologist — and have a much more substantive conversation than you can have starting from a standard panel.

This single piece of information — you can do this yourself — is the one we wish we had had earlier. So now you have it.

What These Numbers Cannot Tell You

We want to close with what labs can't do, because we think the wellness internet often makes the opposite mistake.

Labs are tools, not gospel. They give you a snapshot of one morning's chemistry in one tube of blood. They don't capture how you sleep. They don't capture the brain fog at 2 p.m. They don't capture the way your shirt fits. They don't tell you what you should do — they only tell you what's happening.

Adults inside The Repair Window who get obsessed with tracking labs every six weeks usually find themselves anxious, expensive, and no closer to the answer than they were before. The right cadence is once or twice a year, and the right use of the data is to identify the one or two things that are obviously off and to do something specific about them.

We run our panels twice a year. We adjust one or two things based on what we see. We move on with our lives.

The body is not a dashboard. Don't make it one.

What To Do Next

If you've been told your labs are "normal" and you have not felt normal — book a panel. Use the list above. Bring it to your appointment, or order direct.

While you wait for the appointment, the three shifts in Letter #1 and the three in Letter #2 are worth doing anyway. They will move some of the numbers above before you ever see them.
